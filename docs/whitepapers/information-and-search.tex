\begin{abstract}
MTGallium's policies play Magic without seeing the opponent's hand or the
order of either library. This paper defines what a player knows, how a policy
weighs the possibilities it cannot rule out, what a value estimate means, and
what MTGallium's search computes. Each term it defines is the one used in the
code and in \href{https://github.com/huxinq/MTGallium/blob/main/docs/terminology.md}{\code{docs/terminology.md}};
\cref{sec:code} maps each one to its source.
One small example runs throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why a model}

A program that plays a hidden-information game can go wrong in ways that
still produce reasonable-looking moves. It can peek at the referee's state
through a side channel. It can average over guesses about the opponent's hand
and then act as if it knew which guess was right. It can report a number
from its search without saying what question that number answers. Each of
these errors is easy to state precisely and hard to see in a game log. This
paper gives the definitions needed to state them precisely.

\Cref{sec:game} describes the game and what each player observes.
\Cref{sec:belief} turns observations into probabilities, and
\cref{sec:value} defines values. \Cref{sec:search} describes the search, and
\cref{sec:safety} states what keeps it honest. \Cref{sec:representations,sec:code}
connect the model to the implementation, and \cref{sec:limits} states what the
model leaves out.

\paragraph{The running example: holding up Counterspell.}
We call the player we are planning for \emph{Rae} and her opponent \emph{Oli}.
Oli has one card in hand. It was drawn from a library holding one Counterspell
and two lands, so it is Counterspell~($\Cs$) with probability $1/3$ and a
land~($\Ld$) otherwise. Oli sees the card; Rae does not.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Oli either \emph{holds up} mana ($H$) or \emph{taps out} ($T$). Rae sees
  which. If he taps out, nothing can be countered this turn, so Rae casts her
  threat and wins.
\item If Oli holds up mana, Rae either \emph{casts} her threat now or casts a
  \emph{bait} spell first. She has mana for one spell a turn, so baiting
  delays the threat by a turn.
\item Casting now wins ($+1$) if Oli holds a land and loses ($-1$) if he
  counters it.
\item After a bait, Oli with Counterspell must decide whether to spend it on
  the bait. He cannot tell whether the bait is Rae's best card or a decoy for a
  stronger follow-up, so assume he counters it half the time. If he does, or
  if he holds only a land, the threat resolves a turn late. If he does not, he
  counters the threat next turn ($-1$).
\end{enumerate}
A threat that resolves a turn late has given Oli an extra draw to find an
answer. Say Rae then wins three games in four, so her expected payoff is
$0.75(+1)+0.25(-1)=+0.5$. Payoffs are Rae's; Oli's are their negatives. Whether Rae should cast or bait
after seeing $H$ depends on how likely Oli is to hold Counterspell, and
that depends on how Oli plays.

\section{The game and what players observe}
\label{sec:game}

\subsection{States, chance, and histories}

\begin{definition}[Game]
\label{def:game}
A two-player game with chance consists of
\begin{itemize}
\item a set of \term{states} $S$ with an initial state $s_0$ and terminal states $F\subseteq S$;
\item for each nonterminal state $s$, an \term{actor} $p(s)\in\{1,2,\chance\}$,
  where $\chance$ denotes \term{chance}, and a finite nonempty set of
  \term{legal actions} $A(s)$;
\item a deterministic \term{transition} $s'=\tau(s,a)$ for each legal action;
\item a fixed distribution $\kappa(\cdot\mid s)$ over $A(s)$ at each chance state;
\item a \term{terminal payoff} $u_i(s)\in[-1,1]$ for each player at each
  terminal state, with $u_1=-u_2$. A win is $+1$ and a draw is $0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

A state is the complete configuration, including every hidden card and the
order of every library. A transition applies an action and all of its
automatic consequences, and stops at the next point where a player or chance
must choose. In Magic, a player choice is anything the rules ask a player to
decide: casting, targeting, ordering triggers, declaring blockers, keeping or
mulliganing. Chance covers every shuffle, die roll, coin flip, and random
discard, as well as the opening deal, which is why a single initial state is
enough. A shuffle is one chance action whose outcome is the new library order.

A \term{history} $h=(s_0,a_0,s_1,\dots,a_{n-1},s_n)$ is a sequence of legal
steps. Its \term{reach probability} under a pair of policies
$\pi=(\pi_1,\pi_2)$ (\cref{sec:belief}) is the product of the probabilities of
its actions, where $h_{\le k}$ is the prefix of $h$ ending at $s_k$:
\begin{equation}
\Prob^{\pi}(h)=\prod_{k<n}\;
\begin{cases}
\kappa(a_k\mid s_k), & p(s_k)=\chance,\\
\pi_{p(s_k)}(a_k\mid x_{p(s_k)}(h_{\le k})), & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reach}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Observations and information states}

\begin{definition}[Observations and information state]
Each step $(s,a,s')$ gives player $i$ a possibly empty sequence of
\term{observations} $o_i(s,a,s')$. The \term{information state} of player $i$
after history $h$ is everything that player has observed, in order:
\[
x_i(h)=o_i(s_0,a_0,s_1)\,o_i(s_1,a_1,s_2)\cdots o_i(s_{n-1},a_{n-1},s_n).
\]
\end{definition}

We require two things of the observations, both true of Magic:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(O1)] \emph{A player observes their own actions.} When
  $p(s)=i$, the sequence $o_i(s,a,s')$ begins with~$a$. In particular, each own
  action is recorded after everything the player had seen when choosing it.
\item[(O2)] \emph{A player knows when they must act, and their
  options.} Whether $p(s_n)=i$, and if so $A(s_n)$, are determined by $x_i(h)$.
\end{itemize}
If two situations the player cannot tell apart offered different legal
actions, the menu itself would reveal hidden information. Requirement
(O2) says that such a difference must also appear as an observation.

Observations include public events (a spell is cast), private reveals
(looking at the top card of a library), one's own draws, and the result of
a player's own choices. An opponent's hidden action, such as choosing
which card to discard face down, produces an observation that it happened but
not which card was chosen.

\begin{definition}[Information set]
The \term{information set} of an information state $x$ for player $i$ is the
set of histories that player cannot rule out:
$\Hset_i(x)=\{h: x_i(h)=x\}$. This is the usual extensive-form
notion~\cite{osborne1994}, indexed by the observation sequence.
\end{definition}

In the example, after Oli holds up mana Rae's information state is the same
whichever card Oli holds, so her information set contains two histories, one
with $\Cs$ and one with $\Ld$.

\subsection{Worlds}

A current state alone is not enough to continue a game. Two histories can
reach the same cards in the same zones while the opponent remembers different
things: in one, Rae's hand was revealed to Oli earlier; in the other, it was
not. Oli's future play can depend on that memory.

\begin{definition}[World]
The \term{world} of a history is its current state together with both
players' information states: $w(h)=(s_n,\,x_1(h),\,x_2(h))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[A world is enough to continue]
\label{prop:world}
If each player's policy depends only on their own information state, then two
histories with the same world have the same distribution of future play and
of terminal payoff.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The state determines the actor, legal actions, chance distribution,
transition, observations, and payoff. The actor's information state
determines the policy's action distribution. After each action both parts
of the world are updated from the same inputs, so the argument repeats
until the game ends.
\end{proof}

A hypothetical continuation must therefore be run from a world, not from a
state. MTGallium's search runs simulations on objects called
\code{SearchWorld}s for this reason. Each one carries an engine state together
with each player's represented history and knowledge.

\section{Beliefs}
\label{sec:belief}

\subsection{Policies}

A \term{policy} for player $i$ assigns each information state at which $i$
must act a probability distribution $\pi_i(\cdot\mid x)$ over the legal
actions. A policy can see only its own player's information state. The policy
we assume the opponent follows is the \term{opponent model}, written $\mu$.

\subsection{Belief over a player's information set}

\begin{definition}[Belief]
A \term{belief} of player $i$ at information state $x$ is a probability
distribution $b$ over the information set $\Hset_i(x)$. Its \term{support} is
the set of histories with positive probability.
\end{definition}

Mapping each history to its world turns a belief over histories into a
belief over worlds, and search samples worlds. The mapping keeps the pairing
between hidden cards and what the opponent remembers. Sampling the state and
the opponent's memory independently would produce worlds that no history
could have reached.

A belief is different from \term{knowledge}. A fact is known at $x$ when it
holds in every history of $\Hset_i(x)$, such as the identity of a card the
player saw placed on top of a library and not moved since. Knowledge excludes
only histories that are logically incompatible with what the player observed.
A belief may also assign zero probability to a compatible history, for
example when the opponent model gives an observed action probability zero.
That exclusion rests on a modeling assumption; it is not knowledge. MTGallium
tracks known facts separately from beliefs and uses them to reject
hypothetical worlds that contradict them.

\subsection{The posterior, and why only opponent actions carry evidence}

Suppose the past was generated by chance, by player $i$'s own policy, and by
the opponent model $\mu$, and that under these assumptions $x$ had positive
probability. Conditioning \cref{eq:reach} on the information state gives the
posterior $b(h)\propto\Prob(h)$ over $h\in\Hset_i(x)$. Split each reach
probability into its chance, own, and opponent factors. By (O1), every
history in the information set contains the same sequence of the player's own
choices, each made after the same observations, so every own-choice
probability is the same across those histories. The own factor is therefore a
common positive constant, and it cancels, leaving
\begin{equation}
b(h)\;\propto\;\underbrace{\prod_{\text{chance steps}}\kappa(a_k\mid s_k)}_{\text{chance}}\;
\underbrace{\prod_{\text{opponent steps}}\mu(a_k\mid x_{-i}(h_{\le k}))}_{\text{opponent behavior}},
\qquad h\in\Hset_i(x).
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
A player learns nothing from their own choices beyond what those choices
caused to be observed. Opponent choices are evidence, because the opponent
chose them with information the player lacks.

MTGallium has two ways of constructing beliefs (\code{BeliefMode}):
\begin{itemize}
\item A \term{consistency belief} keeps only the chance factor. It deals the
  unseen cards uniformly from what remains of the known decklists, and
  rejects deals that contradict represented knowledge. It ignores what the
  opponent's choices suggest about their hand.
\item A \term{policy-conditioned belief} is also conditioned on the
  opponent's policy: it includes the opponent factor, reweighting worlds by
  how likely the opponent model was to make each observed choice.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}[Reading Oli's mana]
\label{ex:posterior}
Let the opponent model be $\mu(H\mid\Cs)=0.9$ and $\mu(H\mid\Ld)=0.3$. After
Oli holds up mana,
\[
b(\Cs)=\frac{\tfrac13\cdot0.9}{\tfrac13\cdot0.9+\tfrac23\cdot0.3}=0.6 .
\]
The consistency belief keeps the prior $b(\Cs)=1/3$. After Oli taps out, the
policy-conditioned belief gives $b(\Cs)=1/15$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Particles}

Exact beliefs over Magic hands are too large to enumerate. MTGallium
represents a belief as a set of \term{particles}: complete weighted worlds.
When the real game advances, a policy-conditioned belief updates each
particle according to who acted:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The player's own action, or chance.} Advance the world through the
  step. The weight is unchanged, since own actions carry no evidence
  (\cref{eq:posterior}).
\item \emph{An observed opponent action.} Advance the world through that
  action and multiply the weight by the opponent model's probability of it in
  that world. This is the opponent factor of \cref{eq:posterior}.
\item \emph{A hidden opponent action}, such as a face-down discard. Draw the
  action from the opponent model, using the opponent's information in that
  world, and advance. The weight is unchanged, because drawing the action
  already accounts for its probability; multiplying by it as well would count
  the same evidence twice.
\end{itemize}
In every case, then discard worlds whose new information state for the player
disagrees with what the player actually observed, normalize the weights, and
resample.

\section{Values}
\label{sec:value}

\subsection{A value answers a stated question}

Let $G_i^{\pi}(h)$ be player $i$'s expected terminal payoff when play
continues from $h$ under policies $\pi$ and chance. For a belief $b$ at an
information state where $i$ must act,
\begin{align}
V_i(b,\pi)&=\sum_{h}b(h)\,G_i^{\pi}(h), \label{eq:V}\\
Q_i(b,a,\pi)&=\sum_{h}b(h)\,G_i^{\pi}\bigl(h\text{ followed by }a\bigr). \label{eq:Q}
\end{align}
$V$ is the \term{value} of the position and $Q$ is the \term{action value}:
player $i$ takes $a$ now and follows $\pi$ afterwards, even if $\pi$ itself
would never choose $a$.

A value therefore has three inputs: \emph{whose} payoff, \emph{which belief},
and \emph{which continuation policies}. We call this triple the value's
\term{target}. Two numbers with different targets are answers to different
questions, and comparing them says nothing about which answer is more
accurate.

\begin{example}[Cast or bait]
\label{ex:values}
After Oli holds up mana, baiting is worth $\tfrac12(+0.5)+\tfrac12(-1)=-0.25$
when he has $\Cs$, and $+0.5$ when he has $\Ld$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Belief & $Q(\text{cast})$ & $Q(\text{bait})$\\
\midrule
Consistency, $b(\Cs)=1/3$ & $\tfrac13(-1)+\tfrac23(+1)=0.33$ & $\tfrac13(-0.25)+\tfrac23(0.5)=0.25$\\
Policy-conditioned, $b(\Cs)=0.6$ & $0.6(-1)+0.4(+1)=-0.20$ & $0.6(-0.25)+0.4(0.5)=0.05$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two beliefs recommend different actions. Neither is miscalculated;
they rest on different assumptions about Oli.
\end{example}

\subsection{Evaluators}

Search usually has to stop before the game ends and score the position it
reached. There are two kinds of scorer:
\begin{itemize}
\item An \term{information-state evaluator} $e_i(x)$ scores one player's
  information state. It cannot use information unavailable to that player,
  so every world sharing that information state gets the same score.
\item A \term{world evaluator} $g_i(w)$ scores a complete world, including
  the opponent's hidden cards.
\end{itemize}
An evaluator's output is only a value estimate once its target is stated,
usually implicitly through the games it was trained or tuned on, and that
estimate still has to be validated. Putting a score in $[-1,1]$ does not make
it an expected terminal payoff.

Seeing the whole world is not itself a mistake. By \cref{prop:world}, the
expected payoff $G_i^{\pi}$ of a fixed policy pair depends only on the world,
so a world evaluator that predicts it is legitimate, and averaging its
predictions over a belief gives exactly \cref{eq:V}. A world evaluator goes
wrong when its score assumes the player will choose differently in worlds she
cannot tell apart, which is what a score learned from perfect-information
play, or from solving each world, implicitly does. The next section shows how
much that assumption is worth.

\section{Search}
\label{sec:search}

\subsection{Why clairvoyant choices overvalue a position}

A \term{determinization} is a complete world sampled from a belief. The
simplest way to plan with hidden information is to sample determinizations,
solve each as if nothing were hidden, and average the results. Averaging the best result of each world gives at least the value
of the best single decision:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{w} b(w)\max_{a} Q(w,a)\;\ge\;\max_{a}\sum_{w} b(w)\,Q(w,a).
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
The left side assumes the player picks the best action for each world
separately. That player cannot, because every world in the belief looks the
same to her. The literature calls this error \emph{strategy
fusion}~\cite{frank1998,long2010}.

\begin{example}[The value of clairvoyance]
\label{ex:fusion}
Under the policy-conditioned belief, Rae's best action against $\Cs$ is to
bait (worth $-0.25$) and against $\Ld$ is to cast (worth $+1$). Averaging
those gives $0.6(-0.25)+0.4(+1)=0.25$. Rae's actual best is to bait, worth
$0.05$. The difference, $0.20$, is what knowing Oli's card would be worth.
\end{example}

\subsection{The search tree contains only the root player's decisions}

MTGallium's search builds its tree from the decisions of the player it is
choosing for, called the \term{root player}.
Each tree \term{node} is one root-player information state, together with the
menu of actions search considers there. Different sampled worlds that give the
root player the same information state reach the same node, so the root
player makes the same choice in all of them, just as in the real game. This
rules out strategy fusion within the tree. It does not stop a leaf evaluator
from supplying clairvoyant values (\cref{sec:value}).

Every other step happens inside the sampled world and adds no tree node.
Chance draws from the world's own chance stream (\cref{sec:chance}). The
opponent chooses with the opponent model $\mu$, applied to the opponent's
information state in that world. In the example, Rae's search has a single
node after $H$, with the edges \emph{cast} and \emph{bait}
(\cref{fig:tree}). Oli's card and his response to the bait are drawn inside
each simulation.

One \term{simulation} proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Sample a particle from the root belief and copy its world.
\item At each root-player node $n$, try untried actions first, and otherwise
  choose the action maximizing the UCT (upper confidence bounds applied to
  trees) score~\cite{kocsis2006}
  \[
  \widehat Q(n,a)+c\sqrt{\ln\bigl(N(n)+1\bigr)/N(n,a)},
  \]
  where $N$ counts earlier simulations through the node or edge,
  $\widehat Q$ is their mean result, and $c$ is the exploration coefficient:
  larger values try less-visited actions more often.
\item Continue until the game ends or the simulation takes an edge for the
  first time. At that new \term{leaf}, obtain a \term{settlement}: one number
  in $[-1,1]$ from the root player's perspective (\cref{sec:settlement}).
\item \term{Back up} the settlement: add it to every edge the simulation
  took.
\end{enumerate}
After the budget is spent, search plays the root action with the most visits,
breaking ties by higher mean and then by a fixed ordering of actions.

\subsection{Why the opponent is a model, not a minimizer}
\label{sec:opponent}

An alternative would give the opponent tree nodes too, choosing whatever
minimizes the root player's results so far. Inside the root player's search,
however, every sampled world contains the root player's actual hand. An
opponent that learns from those results learns to answer that hand, which it
cannot see: it would block exactly when the root player really holds the
combat trick. Cowling, Powley, and Whitehouse discuss this form of leakage
in information-set search~\cite[Section~X]{cowling2012}.

Choosing opponent moves with a fixed model $\mu$ that sees only the
opponent's information avoids this leakage. Once $\mu$ is fixed, the root
player faces a single-agent problem with hidden state, a partially observable
Markov decision process (POMDP), as in~\cite{silver2010}. The search then seeks a \emph{best
response to $\mu$}: the root player's best play assuming the opponent
really plays like $\mu$. It makes no equilibrium claim. Search is only as
strong as the opponent model is realistic, and an opponent who plays unlike
$\mu$ can exploit it.

\subsection{Leaves and settlements}
\label{sec:settlement}

A settlement is produced in one of four ways, and search records which one,
its \term{settlement origin} (\code{SearchSettlementOrigin}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Terminal payoff.} The game ended in the simulation.
\item \emph{Heuristic settlement.} An evaluator scored the leaf. This is either an
  information-state evaluator on the root player's information state or a
  world evaluator on the sampled world.
\item \emph{Learned outcome estimate.} A learned evaluator that declares itself an
  estimate of terminal payoff scored the leaf.
\item \emph{Neutral settlement.} The position could not be settled within its
  limits and was configured to count as~$0$. This scores it as a draw.
\end{itemize}
The position scored can be the leaf itself, the end of a \term{rollout}
(continued play under separate rollout policies for each seat, for a bounded
number of decisions or turns), or a quiet position reached from either. A
\term{quiet} position has an empty stack, no combat in progress, no pending
combat-damage or ordering decision, and no creature with lethal damage marked.
To reach one, search takes only \term{forced} priority passes, where passing
is the only legal action. Two optional variants go further: one also takes a
pass that is the only action the configured action space admits while the
position is not yet quiet, and another lets the rollout policies make the
remaining choices. ``Quiet'' is this checklist, not a claim that the tactical
consequences of the position have played out.

The root player's information-state evaluator is used even when the opponent
is next to act. It cannot use the opponent's sampled hidden cards, so the
sampled world affects its score only through the moves and chance outcomes
that the world produced along the way.

If a sampled world refuses an action its own menu offered, the whole search
fails; the refusal is not given any value. Skipping the refused simulation
and continuing would average only over worlds that happen to execute, which
biases the result in a way nobody asked for.

\subsection{What the numbers in a search result mean}

\begin{itemize}
\item An edge's \term{visits} count the settlements added to it. Its
  \term{search mean} $\widehat Q$ is their average.
\item The visits to an edge follow the root player's later choices as the
  search currently makes them, and those choices change as it learns. Their
  settlements may mix terminal payoffs, evaluator scores, and imposed zeros.
  The search mean therefore need not estimate the terminal payoff of the
  action finally chosen, and a visit count does not by itself establish
  statistical confidence.
\item At an edge below the root, the worlds that arrive follow the root belief
  updated by the moves and chance outcomes on the way there, not the root
  belief itself.
\item The \term{root value} is the visit-weighted mean over all root edges. It
  describes the mixture of actions the search tried, not the chosen action.
\item The settlement counts per edge report how many of its visits came from
  terminal payoffs, and how many from each kind of estimate.
\item Stopping at a wall-clock budget makes the number of simulations depend
  on how long each one took. Report the budget that was actually used.
\end{itemize}

\section{Keeping the root honest}
\label{sec:safety}

Search looks at hidden cards all the time: every simulation runs on a
complete world. This is legitimate as long as those worlds were generated
from what the root player knows. The requirement is about where the
inputs came from.

\term{Perspective safety} is the design rule that the distribution of the
chosen action depends on the actual game only through the root player's
information state $x$. It holds when the particles, the menus, the chance
streams, and all random seeds are computed from $x$ and from randomness
independent of the actual game, and every other step reads only these inputs
and what the search derived from them. To see why, run the search for two
actual histories with the same $x$ and the same seeds: every input is equal,
so every step computes the same values and makes the same choice.

Perspective safety is an information boundary. It says nothing about whether
the beliefs are accurate or the values calibrated; those are the questions of
\cref{sec:belief,sec:value}. The practical work is in closing every channel
through which the actual game could leak:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Particles} are built from the root player's represented
  information and the known decklists, never from the referee's state.
  Search checks that every particle presents the same root information
  state and menu before it starts.
\item \emph{Menus} are functions of the acting player's information state
  (O2). A menu that differed between worlds the player cannot
  tell apart would leak hidden information.
\item \emph{Chance streams} are seeded from search randomness
  (\cref{sec:chance}).
\item \emph{Identifiers} such as engine object ids, hashes, and action order
  are information too. Representations passed to policies use viewer-local
  references instead of raw engine ids.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chance inside a hypothetical world}
\label{sec:chance}

The engine resolves chance with a pseudorandom generator stored in the
state. A hypothetical world must not reuse the referee's generator. That
generator determines the actual game's future shuffles, so a simulation that
used it would draw the root player's real future cards. MTGallium gives each
hypothetical world a fresh \term{chance stream} seeded from the search's own
randomness.

For a single continuation, fixing a world's future chance outcomes in
advance changes nothing: an independent seed that only chance steps read
produces the same distribution of play as drawing each outcome when it
occurs. Because the seed is part of the world, and the tree is keyed by the
root player's information state, the seed never becomes something the root
player can condition on.

Repeated continuations are another matter. Within one search, every
simulation that draws the same particle replays its chance stream, so
repeated simulations provide fewer independent chance samples than their
visit count suggests. (When a policy-conditioned belief resamples, the copies
it creates do get fresh chance streams, so duplicate particles do not share a
future.) For a fixed continuation policy this
adds variance without changing the expectation over independently drawn
seeds. That qualification matters: for actions chosen adaptively, and for the
search means they produce, there is no such general guarantee, and extra
visits cannot remove the uncertainty of a fixed, finite set of sampled
futures. Experiments
can also schedule chance streams explicitly, to compare two configurations on
the same random futures.

\section{Representations}
\label{sec:representations}

The program never holds the information state $x$ itself. It holds a
\term{representation} $r(x)$: in MTGallium, the current observation
snapshot, the player-visible event history, the represented knowledge, and,
at a decision, the admitted menu. Three distinct properties matter:
\begin{itemize}
\item \term{Safe}: $r$ is computed from the information state alone. Equal
  information states give equal representations.
\item \term{Complete}: $r$ loses nothing. Different information states give
  different representations.
\item \term{Sufficient} for a task: $r$ keeps everything that task needs.
  For example, it may determine the legal menu, or the value under a
  particular target.
\end{itemize}
A representation containing only the current board is safe but incomplete.
If Oli earlier revealed a Lightning Bolt from his hand in one history and a
Mountain in another, a board-only representation gives both histories the
same representation, and any evaluator using it must score them alike. No
learning method can recover a distinction the input has erased. A complete
representation is sufficient for any task determined by the information
state, and an incomplete one can still be sufficient for a task that does not
need what was dropped.

Representations are compared by digest. Tree nodes are keyed by the digest of
the root player's representation together with its admitted menu, so the tree
distinguishes exactly what the representation distinguishes.

\paragraph{Actions.} Search chooses among \term{semantic choices}: descriptions
of an action in terms the player can observe, such as ``attack with the 2/2
that entered this turn''. Before the engine applies a semantic choice, it is
\term{rebound} to the corresponding native action in that world. An action is
\term{legal} if the rules allow it, \term{proposed} if action generation
produced it, \term{admitted} if it is on the menu search or the policy
considers, and \term{accepted} once the engine applied it. A result should
state which legal actions were not admitted.

\section{What the model leaves out}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Equilibrium.} Search seeks a best response to a fixed opponent
  model. It does not look for strategies that are hard to exploit.
\item \emph{Exactness of beliefs.} Particles approximate a belief, the
  consistency belief ignores opponent behavior, and knowledge tracking covers
  only the events the adapter represents exactly. The adapter reports
  tracking it cannot keep exact rather than guessing.
\item \emph{More than two players.} The definitions assume two players and a
  zero-sum payoff.
\item \emph{Reuse across decisions.} Every search starts from an empty tree.
  Reusing statistics under a new belief would require reweighting, which this
  paper does not define.
\end{itemize}
